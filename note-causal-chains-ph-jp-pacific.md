# Causal chains: Philippines, Japan, and the Pacific

2026-10-02, Claude Code, for Xyh. Companion to
[note-prehistory-as-personal-history.md](note-prehistory-as-personal-history.md).

**Method and limit.** Web search only (10 queries, 2026-10-02), read as search
summaries, not full text. Several hits were encyclopedias, a blog, and business
press. Each chain says what the search supported and which step it did not.
Treat every chain as a lead until a scholarly source is read. Only the list under

**Shape of a chain.** The strong ones have a physical step (volcano, rock,
current), then a market or state step, then a person. The seaway chain has that
shape.

## Chains that touch the places Xyh writes from

### A. Subduction, Negros soil, Iloilo port, sugar (Ilonggo)

1. The Negros Arc formed by subduction. Its stratovolcanoes (Kanlaon, Mandalagan,
   Silay) give volcanic soil and wide flat land.
2. The Iloilo port opened to foreign trade in 1855. Nicholas Loney, British
   vice-consul from 1856, financed planters and brought machinery, while sugar
   prices were rising after the Crimean War. Negros sugar rose from the late
   1850s.
3. Haciendas followed. Negros Occidental now produces more than half of
   Philippine sugar.

Sources: [Negros Occidental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negros_Occidental),
[Nicholas Loney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Loney),
[Sugar industry in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_industry_in_the_Philippines),
[Cambridge JSEAS, Calamba and Negros compared](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-southeast-asian-studies/article/colonial-sugar-production-in-the-spanish-philippines-calamba-and-negros-compared/9247E908CBE71C724902321EDB1BC6AB).
The search also surfaced *Sugar and the Origins of Modern Philippine Society*
(UC Press).

**Not supported by the search:** the movement of Ilonggo workers from Panay to
Negros, and any link to the Iloilo land Xyh's mother owns. Both need a source or
a family answer.

### B. Benguet road, Davao abaca, Little Tokyo, war, Japanese-Filipino children

1. Japanese laborers, first brought for the Benguet road, moved to Davao in
   1903–1905. Kyozaburo Ohta founded the Ohta Development Company. Mt. Apo keeps
   the land fertile, and Davao lies outside the typhoon belt.
2. World War I raised abaca demand and prices, and Japanese capital and migrants
   followed. Ohta held 19,833 ha by 1917. Mintal became "Little Tokyo".
3. At the war's end Japanese nationals were repatriated and families were left
   behind. Many Japanese-Filipino children went into hiding without papers or
   nationality.

Sources: [Dacudao, Pioneer Migrants](https://pssc.org.ph/wp-content/pssc-archives/Philippine%20Population%20Review/2010/09_Pioneer%20Migrants%20In%20The%20Land%20Of%20Promise.pdf),
[Mintal, Little Tokyo of Prewar Philippines](https://www.academia.edu/70334814/Barangay_Mintal_Davao_City_The_Little_Tokyo_of_Prewar_Philippines_1900_1942),
[Philippine Nikkei Jin Kai](https://pnjkincdavao.com/filipino-japanese-descendants-history-travails/),
[Inquirer, Little Tokyo in Davao](https://lifestyle.inquirer.net/254364/little-tokyo-davao/).

This is the closest chain to the question of what Filipino-Japanese prehistory
has cascaded into the present: it runs from a volcano to Filipino-Japanese
people.

### C. Silk price collapse, rural Japan, Manchuria

1. The 1929 Wall Street crash and Japan's 1930 return to the gold standard
   collapsed raw silk prices. Silk was Japan's chief dollar earner and supported
   about 2.2 million farm households, many in Nagano.
2. Rural poverty deepened (the Showa Depression), and the state promoted agrarian
   emigration to Manchukuo from 1932. About 380,000 farmers and their families
   went.

Sources: [MIT Visualizing Cultures, Political Protest in Interwar Japan](https://visualizingcultures.mit.edu/protest_interwar_japan/pij1_essay03.html),
[Japanese settlers in Manchuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_settlers_in_Manchuria),
[Asia-Pacific Journal, Tamanoi](https://apjjf.org/mariko-asano-tamanoi/3032/article).
Kerry Smith, *A Time of Crisis*, came up as a book to check.

**Caution:** this is context for the place, not Xyh's family story. Xyh's
grandmother spoke English and Russian and the father was a child there. Nothing
here says they were agrarian settlers, and the note should not imply it.

### D. The yen after 1985, Japanese investment, and people

1. Plaza Accord, 22 September 1985. The yen moved from about 242 to the dollar
   to about 153 in 1986 and 120 by 1988.
2. Japanese manufacturers relocated to ASEAN and China. Japanese investment in
   East Asia rose from about US$1 billion in the first half of the 1980s to about
   US$8 billion at the 1989 peak.
3. Separately, the number of Filipino women entering Japan passed ten thousand
   in 1979 ("Japayuki Year One"), and the 1985–1990 bubble years left many
   Japanese-Filipino children without recognition. Japan's 2008 Nationality Law
   revision let children born out of wedlock after 1985 gain nationality if a
   Japanese father recognized them.

Sources: [Japan Times on the Plaza Accord](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/commentary/2025/09/22/japan/the-plaza-accord-still-the-gold-standard/),
[RBA, Ishida](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/confs/1994/ishida.html),
[Ubalde, Diverging Narratives](https://asj.upd.edu.ph/mediabox/archive/ASJ-49-2-2013/Ubalde.pdf),
[Made in Japan, ed. Ventura](https://archium.ateneo.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1015&context=phstudies).

**Not supported:** that this explains the family's trade business. The sources
cover manufacturing relocation, which is not the same as importing Japanese goods
for Philippine clients. They also do not give a mechanism for the yen-peso
movement mentioned in the dictation. Ask the family or find trade statistics
before drawing the line.

## Chains with a physical step Xyh may not know

### E. Eel larvae: Mariana spawning, Philippine fork, Kuroshio, Japan

Japanese eels spawn near 14–17°N, 142–143°E, west of the Mariana Islands, about
3,000 km from their East Asian habitat. The larvae drift in the North Equatorial
Current, which splits along the Philippines' east coast into the Kuroshio (north,
toward Japan) and the Mindanao Current (south). They take about 4–6 months to
reach the coast.

Sources: [PLOS ONE, interannual circulation and larval migration](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0144423),
[PLOS ONE, ENSO effects](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0195544).

The Philippine coast is where the current sorts larvae between going to Japan and
not. This relation between the two countries runs through no person. Whether it
belongs in the work is open.

### F. Ophiolite belt, nickel laterite, Japanese smelters

Nickel laterites in Caraga formed from tropical weathering of serpentinized
ultramafic rock in the Samar–Surigao ophiolite segment. Japan's ferro-nickel
smelters rely entirely on imported laterite ore, including from the Philippines.
Japan agreed to buy nickel from Nonoc Island in return for a Kobe Steel plant,
and Nippon Mining explored Palawan.

Sources: [MDPI, Ni(Co) laterite of Southeast Asia](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-163X/14/2/134),
[Development of ferro-nickel smelting in Japan](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0301751687900299),
[Minerals Yearbook 1971](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/APWGLNG3VAWEER8C/text/A5PLGIXVVRLWN78I).
The personal end of this chain is missing.

### G. Mindanao bananas to Japan

The first commercial banana shipment went from Davao to Yokohama in 1964.
Japanese trading companies invested from the 1960s. Sumifru, a Sumitomo
subsidiary, supplies about 30% of the bananas sold in Japan. This shares Davao and
Mt. Apo with chain B.

Sources: [Talakasaysayan](http://talakasaysayan.org/index.php/talakasaysayan/article/view/30),
[BusinessMirror](https://businessmirror.com.ph/2016/11/06/japans-ongoing-love-affair-with-philippine-bananas/).

### H. Exile to Manila

In 1614 the Tokugawa banned Christianity, and Takayama Ukon sailed to Manila with
about 300 followers, arriving on 11 December 1614. A Japanese quarter (Nihonmachi)
at Dilao held 300–400 people in 1593.

Sources: [Nihonmachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihonmachi),
[Takayama Ukon site](https://takayamaukon.com/plaza-dilao-in-history/).
The search did not confirm the wind and current route of ships between the two
countries.
